# James Joyce

**James Joyce** (Dublin, 1882 – Zurich, 1941) is one of the great innovators of
the modern novel. His collection of short stories ***Dubliners*** (1914) offers a
portrait of the lives of ordinary people in Dublin and runs as an
**interdisciplinary path** with the Italian **psychological novel** of
[Italo Svevo](../../letteratura/03-decadentismo/04-narrativa-della-crisi/01-italo-svevo.md).

## Life

Joyce and his wife **left Ireland in 1904**, moving to Zurich and then to
**Trieste**, where he taught English. Among his students was **Ettore Schmitz**,
better known as [**Italo Svevo**](../../letteratura/03-decadentismo/04-narrativa-della-crisi/01-italo-svevo.md):
while writing the last stories of *Dubliners*, Joyce read them to Svevo, who
confessed he too had written novels (*Una vita*, *Senilità*) that the critics had
ignored. When Joyce later moved to **Paris**, he helped to make his friend's
novels known there — and Svevo's late success in Italy came only **after** that
recognition abroad. Joyce spent most of his adult life as a voluntary **exile**,
obsessed with the liberating effects of escaping from Ireland.

## Poetics

When he began *Dubliners*, Joyce was influenced by the Norwegian playwright
**Henrik Ibsen**, who had shown that provincial life could be the subject of
serious art. Joyce wanted to "write a chapter of the **moral history of Ireland**"
and chose Dublin "because that city seemed to me the **centre of paralysis**". Two
ideas are central to his art:

- **Paralysis** — the moral and psychological inability to act, caused by
  political, religious, cultural and economic forces. Joyce's characters are
  trapped by **poverty, social pressure, family duties** and the conformity
  encouraged by the **Catholic Church**: they dream of escape and change, but
  always **fail**.
- The **epiphany** — from a Greek word meaning *manifestation*. It is a sudden
  **moment of self-revelation**, in which a character recognises a painful truth
  about themselves or their life. It is usually the **climax** of the story, and
  it generally leads not to action but to a **fatalistic acceptance** of weakness,
  reinforcing the idea of paralysis.

**Narrative technique:** each story is told from the **perspective of the
character**, without an omniscient narrator and without the author intervening to
comment. Against the action-packed, adventurous short stories fashionable at the
time, Joyce's are **psychological sketches** where action is reduced to a minimum.

### *Dubliners* (1914)

A collection of **15 short stories**, published in 1914 (written before 1905,
except the last and longest, *The Dead*, added in 1907). The stories give a
critical portrait of a **Dublin in decline** — its run-down buildings, its
provincialism, the weight of the Church — and of the lower- and middle-class
people imprisoned in it. **Paralysis** is the key theme running through them all.

The collection is arranged in **four stages** of life:

- **Childhood**, **adolescence**, **maturity** and **public life**.
- *Eveline* belongs to the group of **adolescence**.

??? brano "*Eveline*"

    The short story read in full in class. Eveline, a nineteen-year-old Dublin
    girl, must choose between **staying** to look after her family and **escaping**
    to a new life abroad — and her choice becomes the very image of Joyce's
    **paralysis**.

    ???+ abstract "Summary"

        There is virtually **no action**: most of the story takes place inside
        Eveline's mind as she sits by a window at dusk. She remembers her
        childhood, the neighbours now emigrated or dead, and her hard present
        life: she works in a store and at home, caring for her **violent,
        domineering father** and two young brothers, and hands over her whole
        wages to the family. She has met a sailor, **Frank**, who treats her
        tenderly and has convinced her to **run away with him to Buenos Aires**.

        As she prepares to leave, an **organ-grinder** in the street recalls the
        melody played the night her **mother died** — and the **promise** she made
        to keep the family together. The memory of her mother's wasted life, "of
        commonplace sacrifices closing in final craziness", at first strengthens
        her resolve to leave. But at the **dock**, with the boat ready and Frank
        calling her to board, she is suddenly **paralysed**: "All the seas of the
        world tumbled about her heart… he would drown her." She **cannot go**. She
        clutches the railing and lets Frank leave alone, her face showing **no
        emotion at all** — "passive, like a helpless animal".

    **Temi:** **paralysis** and the failure of escape; **family duty** and the
    weight of a promise; the **idealised past** against a bleak present; the
    final **epiphany**.

    **Significance and themes:** Eveline has **every reason to leave** — poverty,
    an unfair and violent father, a future that may repeat her mother's sad life —
    yet she lacks the **courage and strength** to make the leap to freedom. The
    greatest barrier is **psychological**: she sees her chance of life (Frank, the
    sea, Buenos Aires) as a source of **danger**, and her **choice is not to act
    rather than to act**. The story is a pure example of Joyce's **paralysis** and
    of the **epiphany** that leads not to liberation but to a fatalistic
    acceptance of weakness.
