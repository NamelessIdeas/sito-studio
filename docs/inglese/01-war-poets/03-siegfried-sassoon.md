# Siegfried Sassoon

**Siegfried Sassoon** (1886–1967) is the voice of **anger and protest** among the
[War Poets](index.md). A decorated soldier, he turned his poetry into a bitter,
**sarcastic** denunciation of those who waged the war from safety.

## Life

Sassoon joined the British Army out of **patriotism** and was already in service
when Britain entered the war in August 1914. He soon became aware of the gap
between the **picture of heroism** painted at home and the **reality** the
soldiers faced on the battlefield. Renowned for his bravery on the Western Front,
he became the focal point of **dissent**, especially when he issued his
**Soldier's Declaration** in 1917. He was sent to the
[Craiglockhart War Hospital](index.md) — officially for shell shock, in reality to
avoid court-martialling him — where he met and encouraged
[**Wilfred Owen**](02-wilfred-owen.md). He survived the war and went on writing
until his death in 1967.

## Poetics

Sassoon's war poetry is **bitterly sarcastic and derisive** of the comfortable
establishment that supported the continuation of the war while showing little
concern for those who suffered it. His tone is one of **anger and bitterness**,
directed at those who start wars and send other men to their death.

## Works

- ***Base Details*** — a satirical attack on the staff officers.
- ***A Soldier's Declaration*** (1917) — his public statement against the war.

## *Base Details*

!!! quote "From the text"
    > If I were fierce, and bald, and short of breath, /
    > I'd live with scarlet Majors at the Base…

???+ abstract "Summary"

    The poet imagines himself as one of the **staff officers** ("scarlet
    Majors") who send soldiers off to the front to be killed while they stay
    safely **at the Base**, eating and drinking in luxury hotels. With heavy
    **sarcasm**, Sassoon pictures the well-fed Major reading the lists of the dead
    ("losing" the young men as if at a game) and grumbling about the casualties,
    before going home to die comfortably in his bed — unlike the soldiers he sent
    to their deaths.

**Form:** two satirical **quatrains** followed by a final **couplet** that drops
the sarcasm to remind us that war is **not** a joke but deadly serious.

**Significance and themes:** **anger and bitterness**. The message is that army
officers plan battles from the **safety of their base** and never face the
fighting themselves: they do not know — and do not care about — the horrors they
force the soldiers to endure. The contrast between the officers' comfort and the
soldiers' death is the source of the poem's **savage irony**.

## *A Soldier's Declaration* (1917)

In July 1917 Sassoon's **"A Soldier's Declaration"** was published in several
British newspapers and was subsequently **read out in Parliament**. In this open
letter Sassoon, a serving and decorated officer, claimed that the government was
**unnecessarily prolonging the war** and that the conflict had become one of
"aggression and conquest". He expected to be court-martialled; instead, the War
Office convened a **medical board** which declared him medically unfit due to
**shell shock** — a convenient way to silence such a prominent figure without a
scandal. The text is not a poem but a **public act of protest**, one of the most
famous anti-war statements of the war.
