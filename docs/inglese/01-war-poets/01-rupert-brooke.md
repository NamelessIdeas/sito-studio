# Rupert Brooke

**Rupert Brooke** (1887–1915) was one of the leading figures of the so-called
[**Georgian poets**](index.md) and was already a well-established poet when the
war broke out. He embodies the most **patriotic** and idealistic voice among the
[War Poets](index.md).

## Life

Brooke was caught up in the **initial enthusiasm** for a war which, it was
believed, would be short and painless. His first collection, simply called
*Poems*, appeared in 1911, but it was for the **five sonnets** he wrote during the
early months of the war that he became famous. He did not live to experience the
real hardships of the conflict: he died in **April 1915** of septicaemia, caused
by a mosquito bite, on his way to fight with his unit in **Gallipoli**.

## Poetics

In a rather **sentimental**, Georgian style, Brooke tends to **glorify war** and
the romantic notion of **sacrifice and heroism**. His poetry expresses the early,
unbroken patriotism of 1914, before the mass slaughter of the trenches had become
clear — a viewpoint that the later poets (Owen, Sassoon) would bitterly reject.

## Works

- ***Poems*** (1911) — his first collection.
- The **five war sonnets** (1914), among which ***The Soldier***.

## *The Soldier* (1914)

!!! quote "From the text"
    > If I should die, think only this of me: /
    > That there's some corner of a foreign field /
    > That is for ever England.

???+ abstract "Summary"

    A **sonnet** written in 1914, famous for its patriotic viewpoint and its
    heroic idea of sacrifice for one's homeland. The poet imagines his own death
    far from home: wherever the body of the dead soldier may lie, that "corner of
    a foreign field" will become **for ever England**, enriched by English dust.
    **England** is presented as an idealised **mother** who gave life, form and
    beauty to her son. There is no reference to death as something negative —
    only to the **honour** of dying for England.

**Form:** Petrarchan **sonnet** (octave + sestet), in a regular, traditional and
musical style typical of Georgian poetry.

**Key features:**

- **England / English** repeated throughout the poem, turning the homeland into
  the true protagonist.
- The **idealised mother-country** that gives birth, shapes and blesses her
  soldiers.
- Death seen not as horror but as a kind of **return** and consecration.

**Significance and themes:** the poem expresses the **patriotism** and naïve
idealism of the early war: sacrifice is noble, death is sweetened into an
eternal union with England. It is exactly this vision that
[Owen](02-wilfred-owen.md) will denounce as "the old Lie".

!!! tip "Connections"
    A useful **contrast** with [Wilfred Owen](02-wilfred-owen.md)'s
    *Dulce et Decorum Est* and [Siegfried Sassoon](03-siegfried-sassoon.md)'s
    *Base Details*: the same war seen as glory, as horror and as a swindle.
    Compare with the disillusioned trench poetry of
    [Giuseppe Ungaretti](../../letteratura/03-decadentismo/05-eredita-novecentesca/01-giuseppe-ungaretti.md).
