# War Poets

The outbreak of the **First World War** gave rise to a vast production of writing
about war that had no precedent in British literature: until then, war literature
had been written by **civilians** who observed and reflected on war from a
distance.

## Soldier poets

What was new in the poetry of the First World War is that it was written by
**soldier-poets** who experienced the war first-hand and were personally affected
by its horror. Some survived but were psychologically and physically scarred for
the rest of their lives ([**Siegfried Sassoon**](03-siegfried-sassoon.md)), while
others died in action ([**Wilfred Owen**](02-wilfred-owen.md) and
[**Rupert Brooke**](01-rupert-brooke.md)).

Although these poets are known today as the **War Poets** (or **Soldier Poets**),
their themes and styles vary considerably: from the **patriotism** of Brooke, to
the **anger and protest** of Sassoon and the **compassion** of Owen. As the war
went on, their style changed too. Heavily influenced at first by the conservatism,
romanticism and sentimentality of **Georgian poetry**, the poets later found that
the constraints of tradition could not express the brutality of their experiences,
and began to write in a more innovative, more **"modern"** style.

## The War Poets and Ungaretti

The English war poets share with [**Giuseppe Ungaretti**](../../letteratura/03-decadentismo/05-eredita-novecentesca/01-giuseppe-ungaretti.md)
the central themes of the **futility**, **suffering** and **hardship** of war and
of the sense of **brotherhood** among soldiers. The British poets, however, tend
to be more forceful and brutal in their depiction of war: the condensed,
suggestive style of Ungaretti contrasts sharply with their direct, at times
extremely **graphic** descriptions.

## Craiglockhart War Hospital

**Craiglockhart**, a hospital in Edinburgh for shell-shocked soldiers, is where
**Owen** and **Sassoon** met in **1917** — a meeting that started a productive
period for both. Officially Sassoon, a decorated war hero, was sent there to be
treated for shell shock; in reality his admission was a way to defuse the tension
caused by his public **anti-war declaration** and to avoid court-martialling such
a prominent figure. Owen had been admitted after a series of traumatic
experiences at the front, including being trapped for days next to the dead body
of a fellow officer.

!!! note "Shell shock"
    Active as a war hospital between **1916 and 1919** (after the surge of
    shell-shocked officers following the **Battle of the Somme**, 1916),
    Craiglockhart treated between 1,500 and 1,800 patients. The debilitating
    symptoms of what came to be known as **"shell shock"** could include
    temporary blindness, loss of speech, paralysis, uncontrollable tremors,
    hallucinations, memory loss and recurring nightmares.

Patients were encouraged to stay **active**: it was Captain **Brock**, Owen's
doctor, who pushed him to write, while doctor **Rivers** was known for his
experiments with hypnosis and dream interpretation. Craiglockhart was a leading
institution for the treatment of shell shock because its psychiatrists believed
in **Freudian psychoanalysis** rather than the electroshock and cold baths
practised elsewhere. Both poets returned to service: Sassoon was shot in the head
but survived; Owen was killed one week before the war ended. After the war,
Sassoon was instrumental in bringing his friend's work to a wider audience.

!!! tip "Connections"
    The War Poets run as an interdisciplinary path with
    [Giuseppe Ungaretti](../../letteratura/03-decadentismo/05-eredita-novecentesca/01-giuseppe-ungaretti.md)
    and the Italian poetry of the First World War.

## The poets

- [**Rupert Brooke**](01-rupert-brooke.md) — *The Soldier*: patriotism and the
  heroic idea of sacrifice.
- [**Wilfred Owen**](02-wilfred-owen.md) — *Dulce et Decorum Est*: the brutal
  reality of the trenches and the "old Lie".
- [**Siegfried Sassoon**](03-siegfried-sassoon.md) — *Base Details* and
  *A Soldier's Declaration*: anger, sarcasm and protest.
