# Wilfred Owen

**Wilfred Owen** (1893–1918) is often regarded as the **best war poet** of his
generation. Against the patriotism of [Brooke](01-rupert-brooke.md), his poetry
expresses **compassion** for the soldiers and a brutal denunciation of the
reality of war.

## Life

Owen served as a **second lieutenant** and had many traumatic experiences at the
front that led to a diagnosis of **shell shock**. He spent some time recovering at
the [Craiglockhart War Hospital](index.md) in Edinburgh, where he met and became
close friends with [**Siegfried Sassoon**](03-siegfried-sassoon.md), who
encouraged his poetry. By 1917 the full extent of the dreadful consequences of the
war had become clear, and Owen began to **question** whether all the pain and
suffering was justifiable. He returned to active service in France in July 1918
and was awarded the **Military Cross** for bravery, but did not survive to receive
it: he was **killed in action just one week before the Armistice** was signed.

## Poetics

Owen's poetry is memorable for its **graphic representation** of the hardship of
the trenches and for its **pity** for the soldiers. The language is brutal, at
times **colloquial and direct**. He often starts from the traditional **sonnet
form** but breaks it up irregularly, as if poetry itself could not contain the
atrocities he describes.

## Works

- ***Dulce et Decorum Est*** (1917, published posthumously in 1920).
- Most of his poems were collected and published **after his death**, with the
  help of Sassoon.

## *Dulce et Decorum Est* (1917)

!!! quote "From the text"
    > My friend, you would not tell with such high zest /
    > To children ardent for some desperate glory, /
    > The old Lie: *Dulce et decorum est /
    > Pro patria mori.*

??? note "The Latin tag"

    The title and the closing line quote the Roman poet **Horace**:
    *"Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori"* — "**It is sweet and fitting to die
    for one's country**". Owen uses it ironically, calling it **"the old Lie"**.

???+ abstract "Summary"

    Exhausted soldiers march back from the front, "bent double" and half-asleep
    on their feet. Suddenly there is a **gas attack**: the men fumble to fit their
    helmets in time, but one is too slow and is caught by the chlorine gas. The
    poet watches him **die in agony** before his eyes, "guttering, choking,
    drowning", and the image returns in his **recurring nightmares**. In the final
    stanza Owen turns directly to the reader: if you could witness such horror,
    you would not repeat to eager young men "the old Lie" that it is sweet and
    fitting to die for one's country.

**Form:** based on the **sonnet** but broken into **four irregular stanzas**. It
opens conventionally, with a fairly regular rhyme scheme, until the very last
lines, where the conventions of poetry seem to **fall apart** — as if the poem
"explodes from within".

**Structure (the four stanzas):**

1. Description of the **marching soldiers**.
2. Description of the **gas attack**.
3. The poet's **recurring nightmare** of the dying man.
4. The poet's **message to the warmongers**.

**Significance and themes:** the poem climaxes in its powerful final line, where
Owen defines the idea of the **honour of sacrifice as a lie**. War is not glory
but obscene, pointless suffering — the exact opposite of
[Brooke](01-rupert-brooke.md)'s patriotic sonnet.
